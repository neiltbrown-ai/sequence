<CbHeading>The Thesis: Three Practices, Two of Them His</CbHeading>

<CbDropCap>Brian Collins has built a design practice from nothing three separate times, under three completely different ownership arrangements — and the differences between them are the whole case. The first, started in his parents' garage in Lexington, Massachusetts after he graduated from Massachusetts College of Art in 1982, grew to roughly twenty people in Concord and served Digital Equipment Corporation, John Hancock, and the Perkins School for the Blind. He sold it. The second was the Brand Integration Group, which he founded and ran inside Ogilvy & Mather from 1998 to 2008 — Dove's Campaign for Real Beauty, BP's Helios House, the Hershey's store in Times Square. He owned none of it. The third is COLLINS, co-founded in 2008 with Leland Maschmeyer, which has since been named Agency of the Year eight times.</CbDropCap>

The conventional read of Collins is "celebrated designer runs celebrated studio." That misses the structure. What distinguishes him is that he has occupied every position on the ownership ladder in a single career: proprietor of an asset he built and exited, salaried creative leader of a division he founded but never held equity in, and co-founder of a firm now deliberately engineered to run beyond its founders' hands. Each stage bought the standing to charge for the next one.

<CbPullQuote>"After ten years I was getting lazy. I needed a new adventure." — Brian Collins on leaving Ogilvy, to Forbes, 2019</CbPullQuote>

The firm's current positioning closes the loop. COLLINS today sells itself under the line "Rewrite your worth," offering eleven named programs — Brand Refresh, Reposition, Expansion, Turnaround, Scale-Up, Brand Creation, Architecture Restructure, Value-Market Fit, IPO/Sale, Brand Merger — aimed at helping companies move upmarket and command a premium. That is precisely the maneuver Collins ran on his own practice for forty years, now packaged as a product a client can buy.

The structures we read against COLLINS — premium service, advisory model, co-founder equity — are the In Sequence framework, not a menu Brian Collins ordered from. He did not leave Ogilvy in 2008 to "apply Structure #1." He left because he was bored, and built the thing he wanted with a partner who could run the parts he didn't want to. The fit between what he built and how the structures behave is what makes the case useful.

<CbHeading>Productized Advisory: Naming the Thing You Sell</CbHeading>

The most structurally interesting move COLLINS has made in the last five years is not a rebrand. It is the decision to stop selling "design" and start selling a menu of named, bounded engagements. The firm's own site now leads with a transformation proposition — "Rewrite your worth" — under which sit eleven programs.

<CbHeading>The Co-Founder Structure: Built to Survive an Absence</CbHeading>

COLLINS is not an eponymous solo studio, despite the name. It was co-founded in 2008 by Brian Collins and Leland Maschmeyer, and the division of labor between them is the reason the firm has a succession story at all.

<CbMetrics>
  <CbMetric value="2" label="Co-Founders Since 2008" />
  <CbMetric value="5 yrs" label="Maschmeyer Away (2016–21)" />
  <CbMetric value="8x" label="Agency of the Year" />
  <CbMetric value="~60" label="Team Across Two Offices" estimated />
</CbMetrics>

The test came in 2016, when Maschmeyer left day-to-day duties to become Chobani's first chief creative officer — a five-year run on an executive team through a period of major growth for that company. The firm did not pause. Its Dropbox and Twitch work, its first Ad Age Agency of the Year, and the beginning of its award streak all landed while one of its two founders was running brand at a yogurt company. He returned in 2021 as chairman and chief executive.

Read structurally, three things follow. First, the firm survived the absence of half its founding partnership, which is more than most named studios can claim. Second, the roles are now genuinely split — Collins as chief creative officer, Maschmeyer as chairman and CEO — so the creative principal is not also the person carrying the P&L. Third, the senior bench extends past both of them; hiring a former Pentagram partner as a VP and creative director is not what a founder-dependent studio does.

The specific equity arrangement between the two founders has never been disclosed, and this case does not speculate about it. What the public record supports is the operating structure, not the cap table.

<CbPullQuote>The prior draft of this case called COLLINS founder-dependent and named succession an open question. The record says close to the opposite: the firm ran for five years without one founder, split the creative and chief-executive roles when he returned, and recruited senior leadership from outside.</CbPullQuote>

This is a sequence, not a flywheel, and the distinction matters. A flywheel implies a loop that spins faster the longer it runs. What the record shows is three discrete practices, each a precondition for the next, with a deliberate ownership trade in the middle.

The garage studio taught him to run a business and gave him something to sell. Selling it freed him to take the Duffy and FCB jobs, which put him in front of a tier of client his own studio could not reach. Ogilvy gave him a decade of Fortune 500 briefs and the budget to answer them at a scale no twenty-person shop could — Dove, BP, Hershey's — and he walked out with the reputation while Ogilvy kept the revenue. That reputation is what made a two-founder firm credible from day one in 2008.

The trade in the middle is the part most creative professionals get wrong in one direction or the other. Stay independent too long and you never get the briefs that build a name. Stay institutional too long and the name you built stops being yours to monetize. Collins did ten years, then left — not on a strategic timetable, by his own account, but because he was bored.

<CbSection id="lessons">

<CbHeading>Transferable Lessons</CbHeading>

<CbAccordion>
  <CbAccordionCard num="01" label="An Exit Is a Career Move, Not an Ending">
    <p className="cb-card-text">Collins built a twenty-person studio in his twenties and sold it. Most designers who reach twenty people treat that business as the career — they run it for thirty years and retire out of it. He treated it as a stage. The sale converted an operating business into optionality, and the optionality bought him the Duffy and FCB jobs that put him in front of clients his own shop could never have won.</p>
    <p className="cb-card-text">**The application:** the question is not "how big can this get." It is "what does this asset buy me next." A practice you can sell is a practice with a value independent of your presence in it. Most freelance careers never reach that point, not because of talent but because nothing was ever built that could survive a transfer of ownership.</p>
  </CbAccordionCard>
  <CbAccordionCard num="02" label="Use the Institution to Build an Asset You Get to Keep">
    <p className="cb-card-text">Ten years at Ogilvy produced Dove's Campaign for Real Beauty, Helios House, and the Hershey's store. Ogilvy owned every one of them. What Collins kept was the reputation, the client relationships, and the vocabulary for talking to a board — none of which appeared on Ogilvy's balance sheet and all of which walked out the door with him in 2008.</p>
    <p className="cb-card-text">**The test:** in any salaried creative role, ask what you are accruing that is portable. Credits, relationships, and a demonstrated capacity to handle a certain size of problem are portable. Equity in someone else's P&L, absent an actual equity grant, is not. Take the institutional job for the first category, and be honest that you are trading away the second.</p>
  </CbAccordionCard>
  <CbAccordionCard num="03" label="Expand the Brief">
    <p className="cb-card-text">Hershey's asked for a billboard. Collins came back with a proposal for a retail store in Times Square, which opened and has traded for more than fifteen years. The brief was a piece of advertising with a media-buy lifespan; the answer was a permanent asset generating revenue long after any campaign would have ended.</p>
    <p className="cb-card-text">**The application:** the brief you are handed encodes the client's assumption about what your discipline is for. Answering it accurately is competence. Reframing it — showing the client a bigger problem they did not know they had, and that you can solve — is what moves you from vendor to advisor, and it is the single most reliable route to a different price. It also fails sometimes. Collins's version worked because he could execute the larger answer, not merely propose it.</p>
  </CbAccordionCard>
  <CbAccordionCard num="04" label="Name What You Sell">
    <p className="cb-card-text">COLLINS does not sell "branding." It sells Reposition, Turnaround, Premiumization, Scale-Up, Exit, Brand Merger — eleven named programs with bounded scope, each mapped to a business condition a leadership team already knows it has. Two of them are explicitly attached to transactions: preparing for an IPO or sale, and integrating after a merger.</p>
    <p className="cb-card-text">**The application:** an unnamed service is compared on price against every other unnamed service. A named program is compared against the cost of the problem it solves. This works at any scale — a solo consultant with three named engagements and a defined scope for each is running the same structure as a sixty-person firm with eleven.</p>
  </CbAccordionCard>
  <CbAccordionCard num="05" label="Build It So It Runs Without You">
    <p className="cb-card-text">The firm carries Collins's surname and has two founders. When Maschmeyer left for five years to run brand at Chobani, COLLINS did the Dropbox and Twitch work and won its first Agency of the Year without him. When he returned, the roles split — Collins as chief creative officer, Maschmeyer as chairman and chief executive — so the creative principal is not also the person carrying the P&L. Senior leadership has been recruited from outside, including a former Pentagram partner.</p>
    <p className="cb-card-text">**The test:** if you disappeared for a year, what would still be standing? A studio where the answer is "nothing" has no enterprise value regardless of its revenue, because there is no version of it a buyer can acquire. Partnership, role separation, and outside senior hires are the three moves that change the answer.</p>
  </CbAccordionCard>
  <CbAccordionCard num="06" label="What Wouldn't Transfer">
    <p className="cb-card-text">**The Ogilvy decade.** A ten-year run as a senior partner at a global holding-company agency, with Fortune 500 budgets, is a credential that opens doors cold outreach never will — and holding-company hiring at that level is not an open competition. **The 1990s and 2000s design economy.** Collins built a twenty-person studio at a moment when regional corporate clients bought identity work from regional studios; that middle market has been substantially eroded by in-house teams and platform tooling. **A client roster that compounds.** Dove, Microsoft, Spotify, Dropbox — each name makes the next easier to win, and there is no way to cold-start a list like that. **Financial data is not disclosed.** COLLINS is private and publishes no revenue, fees, or equity terms; this case names the structures and refuses to invent the numbers.</p>
    <p className="cb-card-text">**But the sequence is universal.** Build something with a value independent of your presence, and be willing to sell it. Take institutional work for the portable assets — reputation, relationships, and the demonstrated ability to handle a certain size of problem — and be clear-eyed that the equity is not yours. Expand the brief when you can actually execute the larger answer. Name what you sell so it is compared against the cost of the problem, not against the next quote. Build a partnership and split the roles so the firm can stand without you. These principles work whether the practice is sixty people across two coasts or one person with three named engagements.</p>
  </CbAccordionCard>
